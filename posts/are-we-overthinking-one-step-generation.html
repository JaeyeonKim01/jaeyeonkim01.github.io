Motivation

Building one- and few-step generative models has received considerable attention over the past few years. This includes Consistency Models [1], Distribution Matching Distillation [2], Progressive Distillation [3], Inductive Moment Matching [4], Flow Map Matching [5], and MeanFlow [6]. Their formulations differ, but much of this progress builds on diffusion or flow: compressing an existing sampler, enforcing consistency along a trajectory, or learning the transition across a time interval. Some methods distill a pretrained teacher; others train from scratch using a trajectory-derived relation.

As someone drawn to the mathematical structure of diffusion and flow models, I have found this line of work exciting. At the same time, one-step inference can still require considerable training machinery. Depending on the method, this includes specialized stabilization losses and schedules, classifier-free guidance, Jacobian–vector products, or a separate diffusion teacher and score-based distributional objective.

Does training a one-step generator need to be this complicated?

To take a step back, consider GANs [7]. A generator can simply map noise and a condition to a sample, then learn by matching the generated distribution to the data distribution. This approach has already been scaled successfully, for example in BigGAN [8] and StyleGAN-XL [9], although optimization sensitivity and mode collapse remain important challenges [10], [11]. The useful idea is that distribution matching offers a natural path to one-step generation without a prescribed diffusion or flow trajectory.

\[ \hat{\mathbf{x}}_0=f_\theta(\boldsymbol{\epsilon},c),\qquad \boldsymbol{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I}). \]

Here, \(c\) is a condition such as a class label or text prompt, and \(q_\theta\) is the distribution of the generated samples. Conceptually, we can train the generator by minimizing a distributional loss \(\mathcal{F}(q_\theta,p_{\mathrm{data}})\), rather than teaching it to follow a particular sampling trajectory.

Recent Drifting Models [12] and W-Flow [13] show that direct distributional learning in feature space can produce strong one-step generators from scratch. Their training recipes nevertheless involve substantial compute and additional design choices (see Section 2 of our paper). This suggests that optimizing a distributional objective from scratch may still require substantial training machinery to make it work. This leads to our central question:

Question. Can a suitable initialization make distributional training easier?

Priming Models

The idea is simple: reconstruct first, then match distributions. Before learning the full noise-to-image mapping, we teach the network to recover clean-image structure from corrupted inputs. The same network is then used directly as a one-step generator and refined with a distributional objective.

Reconstruction pretraining

Let \(\mathbf{x}_0\) be a clean training image and \(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\) be independent Gaussian noise. Our simplest corruption mixes 20% clean image with 80% Gaussian noise directly in pixel space, and the network predicts the clean image using a squared-error loss:

\[ \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{pre}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_0,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}\!\left[\left\|f_\theta(\mathbf{x}_{0.8},c)-\mathbf{x}_0\right\|_2^2\right],\qquad \mathbf{x}_{0.8}=0.2\mathbf{x}_0+0.8\boldsymbol{\epsilon}. \]

The noise level is fixed throughout this instantiation. Therefore, the Priming Model does not learn a family of denoising steps or a time-dependent flow matching trajectory.

For our ImageNet experiments, we also add a perceptual loss that matches reconstructed and clean images in LPIPS and ConvNeXt feature spaces.

Distributional post-training

After pretraining, we feed pure Gaussian noise into the same network and treat its output directly as a one-step sample. Since the model has not been trained on pure noise during this pretraining stage, its initial samples are still noisy (Figure 1). Nevertheless, the pretrained network provides a strong initialization for subsequent distributional training.

Given a frozen feature encoder \(\phi\), we match the distributions of generated and real features:

\[ \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{post}}(\theta)=\mathcal{F}(\phi_{\#}q_\theta,\phi_{\#}p_{\mathrm{data}}). \]

For \(\mathcal{F}\), we use well-established distributional losses, such as Sinkhorn divergence [14] and maximum mean discrepancy (MMD) [15]. As a surrogate for these losses, we consider the following formulation:

\[ \mathcal{F}(a,b)=\mathcal{D}(a,b)-\mathcal{D}(a,\mathrm{sg}[a]). \]

Here, \(a=\phi_{\#}q_\theta\) and \(b=\phi_{\#}p_{\mathrm{data}}\) denote the distributions of generated and real features, respectively. The notation \(\phi_{\#}\) means the distribution obtained by mapping samples through \(\phi\), and \(\mathrm{sg}\) denotes stop-gradient. The first term attracts generated features toward real features; the second provides a repulsive self-interaction among generated samples. Different choices of \(\mathcal{D}\) lead to different objectives \(\mathcal{F}\):

  • Optimal transport (\(\mathcal{D}(a,b)=\mathrm{OT}_{\varepsilon}(a,b)\)): \(\mathcal{F}\) is a surrogate for Sinkhorn divergence, our main choice.
  • Laplacian kernel (\(\mathcal{D}(a,b)=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim a,\,y\sim b}[-\exp(-\|x-y\|_2)]\)): \(\mathcal{F}\) is a surrogate for squared MMD.
  • Pairwise distance (\(\mathcal{D}(a,b)=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim a,\,y\sim b}[\|x-y\|_2]\)): \(\mathcal{F}\) is a surrogate for energy distance.

During training, we estimate these objectives using minibatches of generated and real samples and backpropagate through the frozen feature encoder and generator to update the generator parameters.

Pretraining shapes post-training

Reconstruction pretraining makes distributional post-training substantially more efficient, a key factor behind our empirical results. As shown in Figure 1, post-training rapidly turns the initially noisy outputs into clean images: Priming-B pretrained for 160 epochs with perceptual loss reaches FID 2.26 after only 1,250 distributional post-training updates, using the online weights.

In general, longer pretraining also improves the efficiency of post-training. Figure 2 compares networks pretrained for 20, 80, and 160 epochs without perceptual loss, alongside a 160-epoch perceptual-loss variant. At matched post-training updates, longer reconstruction pretraining is associated with lower FID. Thus, the useful output of the first stage is an initialization that can be refined efficiently, even if it does not yet produce good samples.

Why might this happen? Jacobian analysis

In Section 2.2 of our paper, we provide evidence for this intuition. The idea is simple: even when the distributional loss provides useful correction directions, the network's Jacobian governs how effectively parameter updates can realize these corrections.

Let \(h_\theta\) denote the feature representations of generated samples, and let \(v\) be the gradient of the distributional loss with respect to \(h_\theta\). A small gradient-descent step with learning rate \(\eta\) induces the feature update

\[ \Delta h_\theta \approx -\eta J_\theta J_\theta^\top v,\qquad J_\theta:=\frac{\partial h_\theta}{\partial\theta}. \]

Thus, \(J_\theta J_\theta^\top\) determines how the loss gradient translates into a feature update. When the gradient aligns with directions to which this operator responds strongly, the resulting update is larger. Our intuition is that reconstruction pretraining captures useful structure of the data manifold, making generated samples more readily correctable through parameter updates.

To probe this, we measure Jacobian alignment before post-training for the four initializations above: 20, 80, and 160 epochs of reconstruction pretraining, plus 160 epochs with perceptual loss. See Section 2.2 and Appendix C.4 of our paper for the measurement details.

Indeed, as shown in Figure 3, across the four initializations, higher alignment before post-training is associated with lower online FID after the same 12,500 post-training updates. These results suggest that pretraining prepares the network to respond effectively to distributional corrections. The precise mechanism by which reconstruction pretraining shapes the Jacobian remains an open question for further investigation.

Flexibility of both training stages

We found that the Priming Model framework is quite versatile: it accommodates various choices of pretraining and post-training.

Alternative pretraining. Masking also works: simply replacing 80% of image patches with Gaussian noise and reconstructing the image reaches FID 1.87 after 12,500 post-training updates. We also recover clean-image representations from a frozen RAEv2 [16] encoder and compose the student with its frozen decoder. This variant reaches FID 2.34 after 12,500 post-training updates.

Alternative distributional objectives. Starting from the same perceptually pretrained Priming-B-160 checkpoint, we replace OT with a mean pairwise-distance objective or a negative Laplacian-kernel surrogate. At 25,000 updates, they achieve FIDs of 1.95 and 1.85, respectively, compared with 1.38 for OT.

Alternative feature spaces. Replacing ResNet-MAE and ConvNeXt with SigLIP, ViT-MAE, and Inception-V3 yields FID 1.65. FDr⁵ also improves to 6.13, but these encoders contribute to FDr⁵, so that improvement is not independent held-out-feature evidence.

Results across three domains

ImageNet: Class-conditional generation

We evaluate class-conditional generation on ImageNet-1K at 256 × 256 resolution. Priming-B and Priming-L are trained directly in pixel space, with 80 or 160 epochs of reconstruction pretraining followed by 25,000 OT post-training updates.

FDr⁵: quality across feature spaces. Alongside FID, we report FDr⁵, the mean normalized Fréchet distance across five representations: Inception-V3, DINOv2, ViT-MAE, SigLIP, and CLIP. This follows FDr⁶ but excludes ConvNeXt, which both Priming Models and Drifting use during training. The five evaluation encoders are not used to train the models compared here, allowing us to assess whether improvements extend beyond the training feature spaces.

EFLOPs: total training compute. We report training cost in EFLOPs, where one EFLOP is 10¹⁸ floating-point operations. For Priming Models, this includes both reconstruction pretraining and distributional post-training, including feature-encoder computations.

Baselines. We compare with pixel MeanFlow (pMF) and Drifting as strong one-step pixel-space generators, and JiT as a multistep pixel-space diffusion baseline. We also include improved MeanFlow (iMF) and W-Flow (WF), which generate in the SD-VAE latent space. All methods in the table use one network evaluation per image except JiT-H, which uses 200 evaluations with a classifier-free guidance scale of 2.2.

Implication

This section is my personal take, closer to a research philosophy than a scientific claim.

Since the rise of diffusion and flow models, it has become natural to view them as the “right” way to sample from distributions in continuous spaces. From this perspective, learning a one- or few-step sampler begins with a diffusion or flow formulation, then asks how to learn or compress its sampling process efficiently. This has inspired mathematical frameworks that I personally find beautiful, together with training objectives grounded in those frameworks. Their generality is valuable: they offer a principled way to approach sampling across a broad class of distributions.

I would like to take a complementary viewpoint. Do we need all that generality for the domains we actually care about? Our applications are concentrated in a relatively small set of structured domains: images, videos, robotics, protein structures, and increasingly text. These distributions have specific characteristics that a generative model can exploit. A framework designed for broadly arbitrary distributions may carry more machinery than a particular domain requires. I think there is room to develop generative modeling methods that make fuller use of this structure.

To me, Priming Models provide evidence for this perspective in the visual domains we study. Reconstruction pretraining uses the structure of the data to prepare a network for distributional learning. The resulting one-step generator does not require a prescribed noise-to-data sampling trajectory defined by an interpolant or an SDE: reconstruction first, then distributional post-training, can already produce an efficient noise-to-sample map. This suggests that how we prepare a model to learn may be as important as the general sampling framework we start from.

That said, Priming Models leave open questions. Our experiments rely on pretrained feature encoders for distributional post-training, and this dependence may become a bottleneck as we scale or move to new domains. Black Forest Labs’ Self-Flow study raises related concerns about external representation alignment, including REPA: external encoders can exhibit unexpected scaling behavior, and their benefits need not transfer across modalities. Reducing this dependence could be an interesting direction for future work.
